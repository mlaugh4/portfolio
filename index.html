Odin

Project overview

Context

Indeed’s legacy business (hosted job advertising) peaked in 2023. In an attempt to reignite revenue growth, SLT formed a tiger team to build an agentic sourcing and screening tool for enterprise employers. While this may seem like AI herd behavior, it also marked a shift into an underpenetrated segment with a much more complicated service blueprint.

Product

Agentic recruiting assistant that uses screening criteria to intelligently find, screen, and summarize talent, as well as distribute spend efficiently across jobs.

Key challenge

Finding a goldilocks optimal input per job that maximizes trust and accuracy, while minimizing setup bloat, and legal and social risk. Research also ran into the universal tension between buyers and users (recruiters): How might we evaluate utility in a vertical experiencing widespread displacement?

Outcome

  • Led team of designers through complex legal, RAI, and multi-constituent problem space -- from concept through 3 pilot phases, and full deployment in early 2026.
  • Vision for agentic recruiting presented at 2026 World Economic Forum
  • Product is currently top sales priority and used by over 4000 enterprise employers and millions of job seekers

Turnkey setup

Our first challenge focused on adoption. We had to address pressure to keep onboarding lightweight, but provide just enough control and transparency to ingrain trust. An unchanging truth about XL employers is that they live in their ATS and have little tolerance for outside tooling and elaborate config. We attempt to overcome this hurdle by creating a simple, standalone page that hides all of Indeed’s legacy interface, and offering a “hiring strategy” that mimics a planning step and artifact that already exists within many enterprise HR orgs.

Agent customization

Extending this focused setup experience: Employers that opt in for agentic sourcing (“headhunter”) are encouraged to tune discovery and screening criteria. Research found that investment in this phase predicts higher chance of hiring outcomes and positive qualitative feedback. Key tension: Balancing cold start burden with need for control. Equilibrium was found with conversational input paired with intelligent pre-fills.

Mapping key AI value drivers

Odin’s value lay overwhelmingly in the relevance of its candidate matches. We wanted an iceberg product: Dead simple UI paired with sophisticated underlying AI and matching logic.

Given these employers are largely embedded in their ATS, a big part of my job was working with the Head of Machine Learning to define two key moments: Generating default screening criteria (see prior slide), and delivering wow-level candidates with the right proof of fit.

Human-in-the-loop when it matters

Once an employer was up-and-running, our challenge shifted to maintaining performance at scale. Single job management isn’t feasible for enterprise users, nor was a fully agentic system. Trust is the dominant term in the AI adoption function, particularly in hiring where the stakes are uniquely high. While we found employers were quick to outsource top-of-funnel screening and sourcing functions, we found human intervention was necessary for many tasks (e.g. budget management and interview scheduling)

Communicating “fit”

Trust hinged on the quality of candidates we could provide, which put a lot of weight on the candidate summary page. Given they were prepended to the resume that was sent to the ATS, this ended up being the fulcrum of our experience and where users spend 95% of their time. It includes three pages: The summary (pictured), resume, and screening interview summary.

Job seeker: Getting behind the AI facade

Hiring is a marketplace AI arms race. Part of Odin’s value proposition is going beyond the AI-gen resume (a problem we also amplify) to identify real humans that actually have required skills and certifications. This flow was additive and took careful consideration to prevent significant volume decline.

Coordinote

Problem

  • Due to federal retrenchment, many trail organizations have had to cancel projects, disband trail crews, and delay volunteer projects. PCT is cancelling 56 weeks of planned trail maintenance (source). Washington Trails Association lost $800,000 in forest service funding. In Oregon, the Deschutes Trails Coalition lost $200k for it’s summer trail crew, and Trailkeepers of Oregon had $500,000 in federal funding stalled, delayed, or in question. (trailkeepersoforegon)
  • As a result, many organizations are increasingly reliant on public reports as an issue intake channel
  • Current solutions are antiquated, often relying on clunky web forms or applications that don’t work well in low-service areas

Goals

Coordinote is a spatial note-taking app for wanderers. Speed-to-note, overall simplicity, and low-service reliability make it well-suited for issue intake.

  • Extrapolate on it’s core function to makes it as easy as possible for trail-users to log issues.
  • Organizations: Maintain issue coverage/throughput with limited budget.
  • Improve report fidelity.

How it works

  1. Trail-users or crew members discover app through organization or trailhead activation
  2. Issues are logged via text message or direct upload
  3. AI: Coordinote takes the image and location and searches coverage zones for managing organization
  4. AI: Find contact information or a public facing issue submission form
  5. AI: Package report and log it directly with org

Project overview

Context

VR and AR was the hot emerging tech when I was in grad school. In my final year, I linked up with two PhD students to build a system for interventional cardiologists to educate and prepare for complex procedures. We got a small investment from the University and space in a lab that gave us intimate access to practitioners.

Product

In close collaboration with practitioners, we built a few experiences: VR viewer with hybrid interaction model (more later), AR viewer, and a desktop model management portal.

Key challenges

  1. Virtual reality is a big shift from current visualization tech, and it’s introduction to the hospital requires a deep understanding of surrounding players and influences. We used ‘fly on the wall’ observation and contextual inquiry to better understand and document workflows around medical imaging and patient care protocols
  2. One byproduct of this big shift is that novelty of interacting with volumetric data. You can’t naturally use a keyboard and mouse in VR, so we spent extensive time in the lab testing various input models.

Outcome

Partnership and seed money from the University of Washington. Features in Seattle Times, Geekwire, and KING5 news. Pilots at 5 major research hospitals. vr workshop at largest radiology conference in the world. Startup has gone through several rounds and evolved into Dopl.

Sketching was fundamental

Because the team was collocated, we used sketching for almost everything. Not only is it inclusive and fast, we’ve found it easier that digital wire-framing tools to quickly design in 3-dimensions. It was not uncommon for us to go straight into a prototype from sketches.

Cross-platform application map

Before epics, we spend a little more time defining high-level flows and fit/finish. This helps us optimize backend architecture and think about how data and features are distributed between our web app and VR.

Ongoing UXR

We were fortunate to have a team of medical advisors and bi-weekly working sessions at UW Med Center. These tightened our feedback loop and helped us narrow scope quickly. Less-frequent usability sessions were held when epics were completed (controller navigation, new interaction integration, web application, etc).

Pyrus Med

Case study

Touch motion

Interaction design, Usability research, Basic Leap Motion dev, visual design

Challenge

While building Pyrus, it was immediately evident that out-of-the-box VR input systems were unintuitive for practitioners. They didn’t have inside-out tracking cameras and instead relied on controllers. This was fine at the time as Oculus and Vive were catering to gamers, but we found almost everyone immediately put their hands out after putting on the headset. They expected this level of immersion.

We needed a system to balance the precise input you’d get from a DICOM viewer with the human factors and comfort experienced with hand mapping.

”This [controller] would be a deal breaker in a real clinical workflow... too many tools.”

Dr Ripley, MD and expert in radiological 3D modeling

How might we accomodate ‘natural’ gestures with efficient, precise, and predictable input?

Phase 1: Google cardboard

Input: Gaze + single button

  • 👍 Highly mobile: Accessible and lightweight. Good for patient education.
  • 👎 Way too limited. Laggy with larger models

Phase 2: DK2 + Leap Motion

Input: Hands: Pinch, tap, rotate

  • 👍 Hardware light: No controller to learn. Basic controls are intuitive
  • 👎 Tracking: Unpredictable & imprecise

Phase 3: Xbox controller

Input: Analog joystick + buttons

  • 👍 Predictable & comfortable. Lots of input options.
  • 👎 Difficult to extract substructures. Doctors hated it; felt like gaming (see above quote)

Phase 4: Perception neuron glove

Input: Hands

  • 👍 Performant and precise tracking
  • 👎 Huge configuration cost. Clunky hardware.

Winner: Oculus Touch + Leap Motion (“Touch Motion”)

Input: Hands + joystick + buttons

  • 👍 Immersive and intuitive
  • 👎 Button mapping requires coaching. Hand tracking is still imperfect

One-handed radial menu

Challenges:

  • Hiding functionality behind buttons resulted in recall challenge
  • Two-handed menus are slow and require dexterity

Outcome:

  • Faster and easily memorized menu selection flow
  • Fewer human factors issues
  • MORE new user errors
